What Supplements Actually Help You Sleep? An Honest Look at the Evidence

By Sara Walker · 12 August 2026

You have tried magnesium. Nothing. You tried glycine because three people on a forum swore by it, and somehow it made your sleep worse. Now you are sitting there with a shelf full of half-empty bottles and a very reasonable suspicion that all of it is expensive placebo.

I have been in that headspace. Not with those exact supplements, but with that exact feeling: the combination of exhaustion, wasted money, and low-grade embarrassment that you fell for the hype again.

So let me try to give you something more useful than another listicle of 'top sleep supplements.' Let me tell you what the evidence actually looks like, why some people respond and others do not, and why that is not a personal failure.

Why Sleep Supplements Work for Some People and Not Others

This is the part nobody in the supplement industry wants to talk about, because it complicates the sales pitch enormously.

Sleep problems are not one thing. Trouble falling asleep is different from waking at 3am and staring at the ceiling. Anxiety-driven insomnia is different from sleep that is light and unrefreshing. Shift work disruption is different from chronic stress. And the biological reasons underneath each of those patterns are different.

Supplements act on specific mechanisms. If your sleep problem does not involve that mechanism, the supplement is irrelevant to you. It is not that you are 'stubborn to response,' as the Reddit poster put it. It is that the tool and the problem did not match.

That sounds obvious written out, but almost no supplement marketing acknowledges it. They just say 'supports sleep' and leave the rest to your imagination.

What Does the Evidence Say About Magnesium?

I want to start here because this is my corner of the internet and I should be honest with you about it.

Magnesium is involved in regulating the nervous system and plays a role in GABA activity, which is broadly connected to the calming signals in your brain. There is also a real and well-documented relationship between low magnesium status and poor sleep quality. Several clinical trials have found improvements in sleep measures in older adults who were supplementing magnesium, and older adults tend to have lower magnesium levels and absorb it less efficiently.

But here is the honest part: if your magnesium levels are already adequate, supplementing more does not appear to do much. The benefit seems concentrated in people who were actually deficient or borderline. You cannot tell whether that applies to you without testing, and most people do not get tested.

There is also a question of form. Magnesium oxide, which is in a lot of cheap supplements, absorbs poorly. Forms like magnesium glycinate or citrate absorb better. If someone tried magnesium and got nothing, it is worth asking what form they used and what dose, but I will not pretend that changing the form is a guaranteed fix. For some people, magnesium simply is not the lever.

Topical magnesium is a different conversation. This magnesium oil spray from Seven Minerals is one option some people use as part of an evening wind-down routine, though the research on transdermal absorption is less settled than oral supplementation. I include it because people ask, not because I can promise it will solve anything.

What About Glycine?

Glycine is interesting and the evidence is genuinely more promising than I expected when I first looked at it. There are some small studies, including work published by Japanese researchers, suggesting that glycine taken before bed can improve subjective sleep quality and reduce daytime fatigue. The proposed mechanism involves glycine's role in lowering core body temperature, which is part of the natural process of falling into deeper sleep.

But 'some small studies' is doing real work in that sentence. This is not a deep, replicated, robustly established effect. It is a promising signal in relatively limited research.

And then there is the person in the Reddit thread who says it made their insomnia worse. Which is confusing, because glycine is also an inhibitory neurotransmitter in some parts of the brain and an excitatory one in others. Whether that nuance is actually what is happening for some individuals, I genuinely do not know, and I would be making things up if I told you it was. What I can say is that individual responses to glycine appear to vary, and 'it worked for someone on the internet' is not a reliable prediction of what it will do for you.

Melatonin: The One With the Most Evidence and the Most Misuse

Melatonin is probably the most researched sleep supplement available over the counter, and it is also one of the most widely misunderstood.

The evidence for melatonin is strongest in specific situations: jet lag, shift work, and delayed sleep phase disorder, where your body clock is running later than you want it to. In those contexts, it is genuinely helping to shift a circadian signal, which is what melatonin actually does. It is not a sedative. It does not knock you out. It is a timing signal.

For general insomnia in otherwise healthy adults with a normal sleep schedule, the evidence is considerably weaker. Yet the doses sold in the US are often 5mg or 10mg, while the research suggesting effectiveness for circadian purposes often used doses closer to 0.5mg. This gap between what is sold and what was studied is worth knowing about.

L-Theanine: Modest Evidence, Reasonable Profile

L-theanine, found in green tea, has a modest evidence base suggesting it promotes relaxation without sedation. Some small trials have looked at it in combination with caffeine for cognitive effects, and a few have looked at sleep quality. The evidence is not overwhelming, but the effect profile is relatively gentle, and it does not seem to make things worse for most people.

It is probably one of the more reasonable options for someone who suspects anxiety or a busy mind is part of their sleep problem. But 'reasonable to try' and 'will definitely work' are very different things.

Ashwagandha and the Cortisol Story

Ashwagandha gets talked about a lot in the context of stress and sleep, and there is some clinical trial data suggesting it can reduce markers of stress and improve sleep quality, particularly in people experiencing significant stress or anxiety. The proposed mechanism runs through its effects on cortisol regulation.

The evidence is more developed than it is for some supplements but less clean than it would need to be for me to call it well-established. It is also worth noting that ashwagandha is not appropriate for everyone, including people with certain thyroid conditions or who are pregnant. And as with everything else here, if stress is not the mechanism driving your sleep problem, addressing cortisol probably will not move the needle.

The Honest Practical Takeaway

If you have tried magnesium and glycine and felt nothing, a few things might be true simultaneously:

  • The supplements were not the right match for whatever is actually driving your sleep problem.
  • You may be in the portion of the population that does not respond to those specific mechanisms.
  • The doses or forms may not have been optimal.
  • Or it really is placebo territory for your particular situation.

None of that is your fault. Sleep is regulated by multiple overlapping systems, and the supplement industry sells products as if all of those systems are the same problem.

If you want to try something else, melatonin at a low dose for circadian timing issues and L-theanine for a racing mind have relatively reasonable evidence profiles and are not expensive. If you want to revisit magnesium with better absorption in mind, this option on Amazon is one I have looked at for form and quality.

But I would also say this plainly: if sleep has been genuinely disrupted for months, supplements are a thin layer of intervention over what is probably a more complex problem. A doctor or a sleep specialist can actually assess what is happening. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, CBT-I, has the strongest evidence base of anything in this space, including supplements. That is worth knowing.

One thing I want to flag before closing: if you have reduced kidney function or take medications that affect mineral or electrolyte balance, magnesium supplementation specifically needs a conversation with your doctor first. It is not universally safe to add without context.

The Question I Keep Coming Back To

Why do we expect supplements to fix sleep before we have even figured out what is actually wrong with our sleep?

I do not mean that as a criticism of the person who tried magnesium and glycine. I mean it as a genuine question about how we approach the problem. Because the answer to 'what actually helps sleep' depends entirely on a question we rarely ask first: what specifically is broken, and why?

If you have been down the supplement road and found nothing, that might be the most useful thing to sit with.
