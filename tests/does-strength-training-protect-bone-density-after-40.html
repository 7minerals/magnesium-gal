Does Strength Training Protect Bone Density After 40?

Published November 11, 2026 · By Sara Walker

Strength training is linked to better bone density, though it is not guaranteed protection. A UK consensus statement says high-intensity progressive resistance training increases spine and hip bone density, and recommends combining it with weight-bearing impact exercise. Evidence on fractures themselves is more limited, since most trials measure bone density.

What the consensus statement found

The most detailed UK guidance is the Strong, Steady and Straight consensus statement on physical activity and exercise for osteoporosis. It reports evidence that high-intensity progressive resistance training increases bone mineral density in the spine and hip, and that combining impact exercise with muscle strengthening best promotes bone strength. It recommends a combination of weight-bearing exercise with impact and muscle-strengthening exercise to promote bone strength.

The authors use the word "promote" rather than "improve" on purpose, because the evidence is limited. Trials mostly use bone mineral density as a stand-in for fracture risk, so there is less direct evidence on whether exercise prevents fractures, and they note limited data for men and for frailer older people.

What the NHS says counts

The NHS explains that weight-bearing exercise is exercise where your feet and legs support your weight, and gives running, skipping, dancing and aerobics as high-impact examples. Swimming and cycling are not weight-bearing. It describes resistance exercise such as press-ups, weightlifting and gym weight equipment as boosting bone strength because of the pull of muscles and tendons on bone. For adults aged 19 to 64 it recommends muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days a week, working all the major muscle groups, and it says people over 60 can also benefit from regular weight-bearing exercise such as brisk walking.

How the consensus statement describes training in practice

For maximum benefit, the statement says muscle strengthening on two or three days of the week should include progressive resistance training, meaning the load increases over time. In practice it describes lifting a load 8 to 12 times, building to three sets. For people without vertebral or multiple low-trauma fractures it recommends moderate impact exercise on most days, such as jogging and low-level jumping, and daily activity spread through the day with long periods of sitting avoided.

What is still uncertain

These sources cover adults broadly and people with low bone density, not women in their 40s as a separate group, so for midlife specifically the honest summary is that more research is needed. The statement also notes that the benefit of high impact over moderate impact is unclear for women over 60 with low bone density. Anyone with a diagnosis of osteoporosis, a history of vertebral fractures or a history of falls should get individual advice first. The NHS says people diagnosed with osteoporosis should talk to their GP or health specialist before starting a new exercise programme, and the statement suggests physiotherapist guidance at the start for people with vertebral fractures.

Beyond exercise

The NHS also lists getting calcium and vitamin D, not smoking and keeping within alcohol guidelines as part of looking after bone health. Smoking is associated with an increased risk of osteoporosis.

Frequently asked questions

Does strength training increase bone density?

The Strong, Steady and Straight consensus statement says there is evidence that high-intensity progressive resistance training increases spine and hip bone mineral density. It uses the word promote rather than improve for bone strength because the evidence is limited.

Is walking enough for bone health?

The NHS says brisk walking is a weight-bearing activity that people over 60 can benefit from. The consensus statement adds that walking alone does not reduce falls risk or improve strength or balance, and recommends combining impact and muscle-strengthening exercise.

Are swimming and cycling good for bones?

They are good for fitness, but the NHS says they are not weight-bearing exercise, so they do not load the bones in the same way as running, dancing or lifting weights.

Is it safe to start lifting weights after 40?

For most healthy adults, strength training is part of standard NHS activity guidance. If you have osteoporosis, a history of fractures or a health condition, the NHS says to talk to your GP or health specialist before starting a new exercise programme.
