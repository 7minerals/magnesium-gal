Perimenopause and Sleep: What the NHS Actually Suggests

Published November 11, 2026 · By Sara Walker

The NHS self-help advice for sleep during perimenopause is practical rather than dramatic: keep regular sleep routines, keep the bedroom cool, cut back on common hot flush triggers such as caffeine, alcohol and spicy food, and stay active. It also lists CBT as an option for sleep problems and hot flushes.

Start with the bedroom and the routine

The NHS menopause guidance advises trying to get plenty of rest, including keeping to regular sleep routines. Its general sleep advice (from the NHS Every Mind Matters service) adds detail: go to bed and get up at similar times each day, including on days off, and aim for a bedroom that is quiet, dark and cool. If you are still awake after around 20 minutes, it suggests getting up, sitting somewhere comfortable and doing something relaxing until you feel sleepy, rather than forcing sleep.

If night sweats are what wakes you

For hot flushes and night sweats, the NHS suggests wearing light clothing, keeping the bedroom cool at night, and trying a cool shower, a fan or a cold drink. It also suggests identifying and reducing possible triggers, listing spicy food, caffeine, hot drinks, smoking and alcohol. Triggers differ from person to person, so a short diary of what you had and how you slept can show you which ones apply to you.

Caffeine, alcohol and screens

The NHS lists caffeine and alcohol among possible hot flush triggers and says drinking above the recommended alcohol limit may affect symptoms. Its sleep advice says alcohol and nicotine can disrupt sleep and suggests avoiding them for an hour or two before bed, and putting phones, tablets and computers away for about an hour before bed. A warm milky drink or herbal tea is its suggestion in place of caffeinated drinks late in the day.

Activity and CBT

The NHS recommends regular exercise, with a focus on weight-bearing exercise to build strength, and lists it as a way to ease mood changes and hot flushes. It also describes cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), a talking therapy, as something that can help with low mood and anxiety and can also help with sleep problems and hot flushes. CBT is delivered by a trained therapist, so ask your GP what is available where you live.

What about supplements and herbal products?

The NHS advises talking to a doctor before taking herbal supplements or complementary medicines. It also notes that treatments such as HRT can be prescribed by a doctor. Whether a medical treatment is right for you depends on your symptoms and health history, which is a conversation for your GP, not something an article can settle.

Frequently asked questions

What can I do about night sweats that wake me up?

The NHS suggests wearing light clothing, keeping the bedroom cool at night, and trying a cool shower, a fan or a cold drink. It also suggests reducing possible triggers such as spicy food, caffeine, hot drinks, smoking and alcohol.

Can CBT help with sleep during perimenopause?

The NHS says CBT, a talking therapy, can help with sleep problems and with hot flushes as well as low mood and anxiety. Your GP can tell you what is available locally.

Should I cut out caffeine and alcohol?

The NHS lists both among possible hot flush triggers that you may want to avoid or reduce, and its sleep advice suggests avoiding alcohol and nicotine for an hour or two before bed. How much they affect you varies from person to person.

When should I talk to a doctor about sleep in perimenopause?

The NHS pages do not set a cut-off. As a general rule, if poor sleep is frequent, lasts for weeks or affects your days, raise it with a GP. Treatments such as HRT can be prescribed by a doctor, and the NHS advises checking with a doctor before taking herbal supplements.
