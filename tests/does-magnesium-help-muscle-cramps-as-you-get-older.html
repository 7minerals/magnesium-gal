Does Magnesium Help Muscle Cramps as You Get Older?

Published November 4, 2026 · By Sara Walker

Probably not. A 2020 Cochrane review found magnesium supplementation unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention for older adults, and a 2023 evidence summary in American Family Physician concluded oral magnesium probably is not effective for nighttime leg cramps. Cramps are common with age, but magnesium has not been shown to be the fix.

Why muscle cramps seem to show up more with age

The NHS describes leg cramps as common, usually harmless and short-lived, and lists ageing among the possible causes. Other causes it names include muscle strain from exercise, dehydration, later pregnancy, liver disease linked to heavy drinking, and some medicines such as statins and diuretics. Some cramps have no known cause at all. That mix is part of why cramps are hard to pin on one nutrient.

What the research found about magnesium

The strongest evidence is a 2020 Cochrane review of magnesium for skeletal muscle cramps. It pooled 11 randomized trials with 735 people. Its conclusion for older adults was that it is unlikely magnesium supplementation provides clinically meaningful cramp prevention. For pregnancy-associated leg cramps the results were conflicting, and the review found no randomized trials for exercise-associated cramps.

A 2023 evidence summary in American Family Physician, written for doctors, drew on that review and asked whether magnesium is an effective treatment for nocturnal leg cramps. Its answer was "probably not." In people with cramps of no known cause, magnesium did not significantly reduce cramp frequency compared with placebo after four weeks.

One limit to keep in mind: these findings are about older adults as a group. They do not separately test women in their 40s and 50s, so for that age range the honest summary is that the evidence is limited and more research is needed.

Could low magnesium still play a part?

It can in some people, which is a separate question from whether a supplement prevents cramps. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements lists older adults among the groups at higher risk of low magnesium, along with people with gastrointestinal diseases, type 2 diabetes or alcohol dependence. Some medicines, including long-term loop diuretics and proton pump inhibitors taken for more than a year, can also lower magnesium over time. If you take either and cramps are new, raise it with your doctor or pharmacist. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own. What the NHS suggests

The NHS says stretching or massaging the muscle during a cramp may ease it, and that regular calf stretching may help reduce cramps but may not stop them completely. It advises seeing a GP if cramps are disturbing your sleep, if you also have numbness or swelling in your legs, or if a cramp lasts longer than 10 minutes.

What about magnesium sprays?

Sprays are a popular choice, but the available evidence does not show topical magnesium meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels, and a 2017 review in Nutrients called transdermal magnesium scientifically unsupported. Many people use spray as part of an evening routine, and that is a comfort choice, not something the research backs for cramps. Frequently asked questions

Does magnesium help muscle cramps in older adults?

Probably not. A 2020 Cochrane review of randomized trials concluded that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention for older adults. Evidence for pregnancy-related cramps is conflicting, and for exercise-related cramps the review found no randomized trials.

Which magnesium supplement is best for muscle cramps?

The research does not identify a best one. Because magnesium supplementation is unlikely to prevent cramps in older adults, the evidence gives no basis for ranking one form or product above another for cramps. If a doctor has confirmed low magnesium, which form suits you is a decision for that doctor.

Why do muscle cramps get more common with age?

The NHS lists ageing among the possible causes of leg cramps, alongside muscle strain from exercise, dehydration, later pregnancy, liver disease linked to heavy drinking, and some medicines such as statins and diuretics. Some cramps have no known cause.

When should I see a doctor about muscle cramps?

The NHS advises seeing a GP if cramps are disturbing your sleep, if you also have numbness or swelling in your legs, or if a cramp lasts longer than 10 minutes. If you take a diuretic or a statin and cramps are new, mention it to your doctor or pharmacist and do not stop the medicine yourself.
