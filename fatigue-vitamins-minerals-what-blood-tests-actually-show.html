Why You're Exhausted Even Though You Sleep Eight Hours

By Sara Walker · 13 August 2026

You are sleeping. Technically. Eight, nine hours a night, and still dragging yourself through the afternoon on fumes, setting a nap alarm like it's a medical appointment. You have more or less accepted this as your life.

Then someone suggests getting bloodwork done, and suddenly there are actual numbers on a page, and some of them are not where they should be.

This is a situation a lot of people in their twenties and thirties find themselves in. You eat reasonably well. You are not subsisting on gas station sushi. And yet something is off, and fatigue is the thing that finally pushed you toward finding out what.

Let's talk about what those numbers might mean, what the research actually says, and where magnesium fits into all of this, because it almost always comes up eventually.

What Blood Tests Can and Cannot Tell You

Bloodwork is genuinely useful. If someone comes back with a vitamin D level well below the sufficiency threshold, or folate that reads as deficient, or ferritin on the floor, those are real findings. They are worth taking seriously.

But there are a few things worth knowing about how to read these results.

First, 'in range' does not always mean optimal. Labs set reference ranges based on large populations, and the lower end of 'sufficient' can still mean your body is working with less than it would prefer. Zinc is a good example of this. A result that just barely clears the bottom of the reference range is not a cause for alarm, but it is worth noting, especially if there are symptoms that fit.

Second, nutrient levels interact. Vitamin D affects how you absorb calcium. Taking high doses of zinc over a long period can interfere with copper levels. Folate and B12 work together on the same metabolic pathways, and a deficiency in one can mask what is happening with the other. The person in the Reddit post above actually flagged this in her edit, which was a good catch. The interactions between supplements are not marketing copy. They are real chemistry, and they matter.

Third, bloodwork is a snapshot, not a movie. One test at one point in time is a starting point, not a verdict.

The Fatigue and Sleep Disruption Connection

Waking three to five times a night, needing a one to two hour nap in the afternoon, struggling to concentrate: these are not minor inconveniences. They are signs that your body is not moving through sleep the way it should be, and that the rest you are getting is not fully restoring you.

Several nutrient deficiencies have reasonable evidence connecting them to this kind of disrupted sleep and daytime fatigue.

Vitamin D has been looked at in this context quite a bit. The research is not perfectly clean, but there is a credible body of it suggesting that low vitamin D is associated with shorter sleep duration and more fragmented sleep. Whether correcting deficiency improves sleep specifically is harder to say from individual studies, but it is not a stretch to think that a body running on insufficient D is not running well in general.

Folate deficiency has known effects on the nervous system, and fatigue is one of the classic symptoms. Folate plays a role in making neurotransmitters, which is a real reason it might affect how you feel and how well you sleep, not just a supplement brand talking point.

Ferritin, which is how your body stores iron, is interesting because fatigue shows up well before ferritin gets low enough to cause clinical anemia. There is decent research on this. A result in the low end of 'sufficient' range can still be associated with tiredness and poor sleep, particularly in women. This is a case where 'not deficient' does not always mean 'fine.'

Where Does Magnesium Come In?

It is not in the bloodwork results from the post above, because her magnesium came back sufficient. That is worth respecting. Not every fatigue story is a magnesium story.

But magnesium comes up in this conversation for a reason. It is involved in hundreds of enzyme reactions, including ones that affect energy production and muscle function. It plays a role in regulating the nervous system and in sleep specifically, partly through its effect on GABA, which is the neurotransmitter that helps your brain quiet down.

There is also the question of whether standard serum magnesium tests tell the whole picture. Most of the magnesium in your body is inside cells, not floating in blood. Serum magnesium can look fine while intracellular magnesium is lower. This is genuinely debated in research, and I am not going to pretend there is a clean answer, because there is not.

What I will say is that if you are exploring magnesium as part of your own fatigue puzzle, topical magnesium is one approach some people try alongside or instead of oral supplementation. The absorption evidence for topical forms is less robust than for oral, to be honest, but some people find it easier on their digestion. Seven Minerals makes a magnesium oil spray if you want to look at what a topical option actually looks like. And if oral magnesium glycinate is more your speed, there is a well-regarded option available on Amazon that tends to be gentler on the stomach than older forms like magnesium oxide.

One caution worth stating clearly: if you have reduced kidney function, magnesium supplementation carries real risks because your kidneys manage magnesium excretion. Same goes for certain heart medications and some antibiotics. This is not a supplement to layer on without checking if you have any of those situations going on.

The Part About Doing Your Research First

The second edit in the original post was the best part of it. Do your research on timing and interactions before you start stacking supplements.

This is not paranoia. It is just sensible. Fat-soluble vitamins like vitamin D accumulate. Zinc and copper compete for absorption. Some supplements interfere with medication absorption if taken at the same time. These are not obscure concerns. They are well-documented, and a bit of reading before you start is worth more than any individual supplement.

The good news is that this kind of information is genuinely findable. Published guidance from health authorities, studies on PubMed, your pharmacist if you are on any medications. You do not need a degree to read a summary of what competes with what.

What Actually Moves the Needle on Fatigue

Here is an honest summary of where the evidence sits.

Correcting a genuine deficiency, confirmed by blood test, frequently does improve how people feel. This is probably the most reliable thing you can do in the supplement space, because you are not guessing. You have a number, you have a threshold, and you are filling a real gap.

Taking supplements on top of already-sufficient levels is where the evidence gets much thinner. More is not always better, and in some cases more is actively worse. Vitamin D toxicity is real. Excess zinc causes problems. The marketing incentive in the supplement industry runs hard in the direction of 'take more,' and the research does not always follow.

Nutrient status is also only one variable in fatigue. Sleep architecture, stress load, how much light you get in the morning, whether you are moving enough but not too much, underlying health conditions, medication side effects: these all live in the same neighborhood. Blood tests are a useful map, but the territory is bigger than the map.

A Practical Starting Point

If you are dealing with persistent fatigue that does not match your sleep hours, getting actual bloodwork is a reasonable first step. Not because supplements are the answer to everything, but because knowing whether there is a deficiency means you are not guessing.

If your results come back genuinely low in something, work with whoever ordered the tests to understand what the numbers mean in your specific context, not just compared to the reference range.

If everything comes back normal and you are still exhausted, that is also information, and it probably means the answer is elsewhere.

The person in the original post did something sensible: she tested first, supplemented what was actually low, and paid attention to how the pieces interact. That is a better approach than most supplement content on the internet will ever suggest, because it is slower and less dramatic than 'I tried this and everything changed overnight.'

I am genuinely curious about one thing, though. For those of you who have had bloodwork done and corrected a deficiency: did the improvement show up gradually, or was it more of a threshold moment where things just shifted? I have heard both, and I have not seen good research on why that would vary so much from person to person.
